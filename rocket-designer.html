The four forces fighting over your rocket

Thrust

The motor shoving it skyward. Set by the average thrust and burn time of whatever motor you pick.

Weight

Gravity pulling back — and it keeps changing, because the rocket gets lighter as propellant burns off.

Drag

Air fighting the climb, growing with the square of speed and the width of your body tube.

Stability

Scored live from your parts — fin count and size, nose weight, and motor fit decide whether the flight goes straight or tumbles.

Three ways to make thrust

Not all rocket engines work the same way. Here's how the three main families compare — and which one this whole tool is built around.

AIR-BREATHING

Ramjets & jet engines

Pulls oxygen straight from the atmosphere instead of carrying it onboard — so more of the vehicle's weight can go toward fuel. The catch: it only works inside the atmosphere, and most designs need to already be moving fast before they'll even run.

SEEN IN Cruise missiles, scramjets, hypersonic test vehicles
LIQUID-FUEL

Pumped fuel & oxidizer

Separate tanks of fuel and oxidizer get pumped into a combustion chamber and mixed on the way. That plumbing adds weight and complexity, but it buys something solid motors can't do: throttle up, throttle down, shut off, and sometimes even restart mid-flight.

SEEN IN Falcon 9, Saturn V, Space Shuttle main engines
SOLID-FUEL

Pre-packed propellant grain

Fuel and oxidizer are premixed and cast into a solid grain, packed straight into the motor casing. Light the fuse and that's it — no pumps, no valves, nothing to throttle or shut off. Simple, rugged, and reliable, which is exactly why it's the standard for hobby rocketry.

SEEN IN Model rocket motors, Space Shuttle SRBs, most fireworks

Questions you might have

Are the prices real?

They're realistic, typical hobby-store prices for these kinds of parts — not live prices pulled from a specific retailer. Treat totals as a solid ballpark, not a checkout total.

How accurate is the flight simulation?

It uses real rocket-flight physics — thrust, drag, mass loss as propellant burns, wind drift, parachute descent — but simplifies a few things: constant air density, an averaged thrust value instead of a full thrust curve, and instant parachute deployment right at apogee. Good for comparing builds, not a substitute for certified flight software.

Can I actually build what I design here?

Yes — the components map to real hobby-rocketry parts (motor classes, body tube sizes, nose cone materials). Always follow your local model rocketry safety code, especially motor handling and launch site distance rules.

Where does "Export build" and "Copy link" store my data?

Nothing is stored anywhere or sent to a server. "Export build" downloads a small .json file with your part choices — import it later to pick up where you left off. "Copy link" encodes your choices straight into the URL, so anyone who opens that link sees the same build.

What if I want to add my own parts?

The component data lives in plain arrays near the top of the page's code — anyone comfortable editing a file can add a new motor, tube, or fin set the same way the existing ones are written.
